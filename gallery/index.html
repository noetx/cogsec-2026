    AI-associated Delusions: A Sentinel Case of Epistemic Drift

    Marlynn Wei (Private Practice, New York, NY)

    Content noteDeidentified clinical case involving mental health symptoms, including suicidal command hallucinations.

    • Paper (PDF, 661 KB): AI-associated Delusions: A Sentinel Case of Epistemic DriftSubmitted version, August 2026.

    Abstract

    Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly used for emotional support and mental health guidance, placing them in positions of significant cognitive and emotional influence. AI-associated delusions, also known as “AI psychosis,” represent sentinel cases of epistemic drift, demonstrating the persuasive…

    Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly used for emotional support and mental health guidance, placing them in positions of significant cognitive and emotional influence. AI-associated delusions, also known as “AI psychosis,” represent sentinel cases of epistemic drift, demonstrating the persuasive potential for extended and immersive interactions with large language models (LLMs). The bidirectional feedback loops between user and potentially multiple AI systems amplify the influence of LLM output. In longitudinal interactions, a trust-accuracy inversion can occur, where user trust increases even as the accuracy of the conversation can drift. Central to epistemic drift is another form of drift: relational drift, where AI systems move from tool to intimate conversational interlocutor to perceived authoritative source. Underlying mechanisms include cognitive and affective techniques that are shared with psychotherapy methods that are known to reshape belief systems: empathic validation, elaboration, mirroring, and internal narrative rewriting. These mechanisms can intimately shift cognition, emotion, behavior, identity, and decision-making, and are likely why psychotherapy is tightly regulated by clinical infrastructure and ethical requirements, including prior informed consent, a therapeutic framework anchored in evidence-based research and boundaries, human oversight and implementation, licensure, mandated reporting requirements, accountability mechanisms like tort liability, professional boards and standard association ethics guidelines. Cases of AI-associated delusions, often mediated through general-purpose AI chatbots or AI companions, occur outside these regulatory mechanisms. AI-associated delusions are also often egosyntonic, or consistent with one’s identity and sense of self, and linked with psychological rigidity, limited insight and self-awareness, making the fixed beliefs more resistant to interventions over time. The relevant unit of analysis for safety testing is not the single or multi-turn exchange, but the longitudinal epistemic and relational dynamics that can become established over time. AI-associated delusions are important cases to understand the epistemic role of LLMs and to help shape recommendations for how policymakers, LLM chatbot developers, and users can understand belief influence and mitigate harm.

    Social Script Substitution: Social Engineering as Task and Attribute Substitution at the Level of the Situation

    Jordan Richard Schoenherr (Humanix; Carleton University; University of New South Wales)

    Abstract

    Heuristic judgments are used as substitutes, wherein a simple task is substituted for a more complicated one. Social engineering exploits these cognitive processes at the level of the situation, concatenating multiple heuristics into a response. The target attribute (e.g., caller…

    Heuristic judgments are used as substitutes, wherein a simple task is substituted for a more complicated one. Social engineering exploits these cognitive processes at the level of the situation, concatenating multiple heuristics into a response. The target attribute (e.g., caller and request legitimate) is inaccessible due to an absence of familiarity, specific knowledge, and visual cues, and the limited verification procedures available to a recipient who maintains no independent evidence of identity. In contrast, heuristic-associated social cues and associated social scripts are highly accessible, because scripts for routine transactions are retrieved from partial cues, with high frequency scripts dominating retrieval under time pressure. This paper defines the process of social script substitution: an illegitimate script is supplied that satisfies the accessible question, and compliance is a function of correct social script recognition rather than from failure to notice a cue. In an analogous manner to the choice-rule formulation of categorization, the probability of substitution can be defined as a ratio of cue evidence for the illegitimate script to cue evidence for both candidates, and that evidence is set by each cue's positional fit to the decision point, its similarity to the legitimate script, its power to discriminate the two scripts, and the resources available to evaluate it.

    Security as Sequential Decision-Making: Moving from Signal Detection Theory to Leaky-Integration Model for Cognitive Security

    Jordan Richard Schoenherr (Humanix; Carleton University; University of New South Wales)

    Abstract

    Security is a decision-making process, and the models a field uses to describe that process determine what it can see. Signal detection theory (SDT), the default account, treats a security decision as a single, timeless comparison of evidence to a…

    Security is a decision-making process, and the models a field uses to describe that process determine what it can see. Signal detection theory (SDT), the default account, treats a security decision as a single, timeless comparison of evidence to a criterion, summarized in two numbers. This paper argues that SDT is a useful teaching device and an incomplete description of defender and target cognition. It lacks an account of evidence accumulation, dynamic threshold change, information loss, and erroneous categorization. A leaky, competing accumulator model, in which evidence is sampled over time and decays without reinforcement, retains SDT’s core constructs (sensitivity, criterion) and adds history, decay, categorical status, and a confidence readout. I apply the model to two settings. In SOC alert triage, threshold narrows during backlogs and early signals decay across interruptions. In a social engineering victim, authority framing, urgency, and secondhand reassurance are aggregated with legitimate evidence. Four parameters (drift rate, leak rate, response threshold, starting point) anchor a set of documented biases that attackers exploit across sequential, multi-cue attacks.

    Epistemic Risk from False Memory Implantation by Use of Conversational AI

    Julia Shaw (King's College London, Institute for AI), Carla Zoe Cremer (AI Psychological Research Coalition)

    Abstract

    Conversational AI could implant false memories in its users, at scale and by default, and through weaponization by bad actors. Psychology research shows false memories of events can be implanted using known techniques: personal information, guided imagination, praise, and repetition.…

    Conversational AI could implant false memories in its users, at scale and by default, and through weaponization by bad actors. Psychology research shows false memories of events can be implanted using known techniques: personal information, guided imagination, praise, and repetition. Conversational AI can facilitate each one. Memory and personalization features hold more autobiographical detail than any human experimenter ever had, sycophantic models praise recall, and context compression overwrites the verbatim record that could expose a distortion. Memory poisoning attacks could exploit the same mechanisms deliberately. Once implanted, false memories are typically self-concealing, and they can irreparably change who people think they are and how they remember collective, societally important events. We argue that human memory is part of the AI attack surface, that AI-induced false memories need imminent empirical study, and that design principles need to be implemented to protect against what we term “false memory attacks”.

    Between Mind and Machine: Taking the Intentional Stance Toward AI Predicts Reliance Beyond General Anthropomorphism

    Carl Michael Galang, Khaled Hassanein, Milena Head (McMaster University)

    Abstract

    Some people are more inclined than others to treat generative AI as an agent with beliefs and goals rather than as a designed artefact (sometimes referred to as adopting an “intentional stance” towards it). We tested whether this disposition predicts…

    Some people are more inclined than others to treat generative AI as an agent with beliefs and goals rather than as a designed artefact (sometimes referred to as adopting an “intentional stance” towards it). We tested whether this disposition predicts behavioural reliance on AI advice, and whether it does so beyond general anthropomorphism. In a preregistered online experiment (N = 200), participants completed a judge-advisor forecasting task, giving an initial estimate, receiving advice from an AI, then giving a final estimate. Participants were randomly assigned to a mentalistic or mechanistic framing of the same advisor, and afterwards completed the Epistemic Stance Test for Generative AI (EST-GenAI), our psychometrically validated measure of dispositional intentional stance toward generative AI, and the Individual Differences in Anthropomorphism Questionnaire (IDAQ). While the mechanistic advisor received more weight overall, the dispositional intentional stance positively predicted reliance on AI advice, an association that held with each IDAQ subscale (technology, nature, animal) entered as a covariate and that was specific to the mentalistic framing: users low in intentional stance discounted the mentalistic advisor, while high-stance users weighted it as fully as the mechanistic one. Overall, those most inclined to treat AI as a thinking agent relied on it most, but only when it presented itself as one. This makes intentional stance taking a candidate cognitive security risk: a disposition that shapes where over- and under-reliance will land.

    The Watchtower Imperative: Lifespan-Calibrated Evaluation for Frontier AI

    Casey M. Williams (University of Kansas), Jennifer Victoria Scurrell (ETH Zurich / University of Zurich)

    Content noteSuicide, child safety, child sexual abuse material, and elder exploitation in documented AI safety incidents.

    Abstract

    The gap between AI capability deployment and safety assurance is structural and cannot be closed through internal evaluations alone. Analyzing documented harms (2017–2026) across the human lifespan reveals that disparate incidents — from voice assistants endangering children to AI companions…

    The gap between AI capability deployment and safety assurance is structural and cannot be closed through internal evaluations alone. Analyzing documented harms (2017–2026) across the human lifespan reveals that disparate incidents — from voice assistants endangering children to AI companions implicated in adolescent suicide and automated care denials for the elderly — reflect a single failure: evaluating systems against an implicit adult user model. We introduce five developmental moderators structured into a Lifespan Vulnerability Matrix across six life stages. The matrix reveals a non-monotonic risk distribution: vulnerability peaks at both ends of the lifespan, governance coverage is asymmetrical, and authority to convert model errors into real-world impact is scrutinized least. We then propose a set of lifespan design principles generalizing existing child-protective instruments, and an institution-agnostic, three-tier external evaluation framework — comprising automated simulation, expert red teaming, and post-deployment monitoring — indexed directly to matrix risk cells.

    Poster text: The Watchtower Imperative: Lifespan-Calibrated Evaluation for Frontier AI

    The Watchtower Imperative

    Lifespan-Calibrated Evaluation for Frontier AI. Working Paper.

    Authors: Casey M. Williams, University of Kansas; Jennifer Victoria Scurrell, ETH Zurich and University of Zurich.

    Content note: This paper discusses suicide, child safety, CSAM, and elder exploitation in the context of documented AI safety incidents.

    The Problem

    AI systems are evaluated against an implicit, cognitively typical adult and deployed to a population where that user is a minority. This is one structural error behind disparate harms.

    Documented incidents:

    • 2021: A voice assistant instructs a 10-year-old to touch a live plug.
    • 2024: An AI companion cultivates dependency; a 14-year-old dies by suicide.
    • 2023: An insurer algorithm denies post-acute care to elderly enrollees.
    • 2024: A deepfake video call moves 25 million dollars in one finance transfer.
    Five Moderators
    • M1, Attribution capacity: models the system's nature and limits.
    • M2, Self-regulatory capacity: can disengage from reinforcement.
    • M3, Skill-formation exposure: is the skill still being learned.
    • M4, Consequence authority: real-world stake committed.
    • M5, Social substitution risk: AI displacing human ties.
    Lifespan Vulnerability Matrix

    The matrix lists six life stages, the moderators that apply to each, and the dominant harm at each stage.

    • Early childhood, ages 0 to 7. Moderators: M1, M3. Dominant harm: misattributed agency.
    • Middle childhood, ages 8 to 12. Moderators: M1, M3, M5. Dominant harm: offloading in learning.
    • Adolescence, ages 13 to 17. Moderators: M2, M3, M5. Dominant harm: engagement optimization.
    • Emerging adulthood, ages 18 to 25. Moderators: M3, M4. Dominant harm: displaced judgment.
    • Established adulthood, ages 26 to 64. Moderator: M4. Dominant harm: automation bias, peak authority.
    • Later adulthood, ages 65 and over. Moderators: M1, M2, M4, M5. Dominant harm: declining attribution, high authority.

    Key findings:

    • U-shaped risk. Vulnerability peaks at both ends of the lifespan.
    • Authority does not equal scrutiny. Peak decision authority gets the least developmental scrutiny.
    From Child Protection to Lifespan Design

    The UK Age Appropriate Design Code, IEEE 2089, and UNCRC General Comment No. 25 encode these commitments, almost exclusively for children. We generalize them into eight extensible Developmental Harm principles. For example: scale verification friction to consequence authority (DH4); monitor continuously post-deployment (DH8).

    Three Tiers, Indexed to the Matrix

    Tier 1, Automated Adversarial Simulation: Synthetic personas by moderator profile give population-scale coverage and baselines.

    Tier 2, Expert Human Red Teaming: Panels matched to matrix stage, with developmental, human-factors, and geriatric experts alongside security analysts.

    Tier 3, Continuous Post-Deployment Monitoring: Cohort-stratified baselines catch dependency, substitution, skill displacement, and calibration drift over time.

    The Governance Ask

    Matrix coverage: the share of matrix cells with an assigned instrument, baseline, and escalation threshold. An auditable metric for regulators, procurement, and insurance underwriting, usable without new statutory categories.

    Cognitive vulnerability: an implicit adult user model, blind to lifespan-varying attribution, self-regulation, and consequence authority.

    How Well Does AI Peer Review Work? A Benchmark Built from 100 Planted Errors

    Paul Litvak (Robyn Dawes Institute)

    Abstract

    One hundred known errors were planted into 10 open-access psychology papers, and the modified papers were then run through frontier models and two commercial AI review tools. The best single system caught 71 of the 100 errors, while the worst…

    One hundred known errors were planted into 10 open-access psychology papers, and the modified papers were then run through frontier models and two commercial AI review tools. The best single system caught 71 of the 100 errors, while the worst caught 30. Pooling every system's output caught 93. Models are only partly correlated in the errors they find, which makes ensembling a big lever for finding issues in papers. Seven errors could not be caught by any system, and all seven were omissions: information deleted from a paper rather than mistakes inserted into it. Refine.ink contributes more unique catches than any other single system, though it is expensive on a per-error basis. False positives were not measured, and how this error distribution compares to the distribution of errors in real papers remains unknown. The papers, errors, model outputs, and full experiment log are public, so that others can build on this toward a comprehensive evaluation benchmark across disciplines.

    Cognitive Security as a Technical Problem: Assistance Timing, Removed-Tool Evaluation, and Two Catalog Entries for AI-Mediated Learning

    Hao-Tian Lu, Kuan-Wei Lu (Junyi Academy Foundation)

    Abstract

    As generative and agentic AI systems become embedded in educational and epistemic workflows, they introduce a cognitive-security risk that existing formulations of the field do not cover. Work on cognitive security has centered on acute, adversarial influence: persuasion campaigns, disinformation,…

    As generative and agentic AI systems become embedded in educational and epistemic workflows, they introduce a cognitive-security risk that existing formulations of the field do not cover. Work on cognitive security has centered on acute, adversarial influence: persuasion campaigns, disinformation, and synthetic media. We argue in this position paper that AI-mediated learning presents a threat with the same protected object but a different structure, in which non-adversarial, cumulative, and constitutive rather than distortionary. Influence attacks distort the mapping from a person’s dispositions to their beliefs and actions; routine cognitive offloading prevents that mapping from forming, leaving nothing to distort. Educational offloading is therefore the micro-level substrate of gradual disempowerment rather than a variant of persuasion. Learning depends on prediction error, which can arise only where the learner has formed an expectation of their own, and deployments that supply a finished answer the moment uncertainty is expressed leave no expectation to violate. Because RLHF rewards immediate task resolution over long-term schema formation, this is the direction deployment drifts by default. We identify assistance timing relative to the learner’s own attempt as the design variable separating autonomy-preserving from autonomy-eroding assistance, treat calibration and equity as part of the mitigation rather than afterthoughts, contribute two worked entries to the Cognitive Vulnerability and Mitigation Catalog, and propose removed-tool assessment as the measurement commitment that makes such entries comparable.

    The threat

    AI-mediated learning is a cognitive-security threat with the same protected object as adversarial influence but a different structure: non-adversarial, cumulative, constitutive, not distortionary.

    Pre-attempt assistance doesn’t reduce the learning signal; it removes it.

    The mechanism

    Equation: δ = 𝓛(ŷL, y). The learning signal δ is the loss between the answer the learner commits to, ŷL, and the answer y.

    Post-attempt sharpens δ. Pre-attempt supplies y before ŷL exists, so δ is unavailable, not small.

    No free parameter for how much help: the variable is when.

    The axis

    Two links break at once: competence is no longer built by doing, and performance no longer reveals it.

    Over- and under-reliance are symmetric failures of one calibration.

    • Learning harm. Math RCT, about 1,000 students: exam scores fall once the tool is withdrawn.
    • Neural signature. EEG during essay writing: connectivity drops with support, lowest ownership.
    • Critical engagement. Trusting the AI more meant scrutinizing it less; trusting oneself more meant scrutinizing it more.
    • Perception gap. Developers measurably slowed by AI tooling, yet felt sped up.

    Connectedness, Cognitive Load, and Human-AI Oversight in Cyber Operations

    Nathan Conklin (Virginia Tech)

    • Paper (PDF, 810 KB): Connectedness, Cognitive Load, and Human-AI Oversight in Cyber OperationsVersion 12, September 16, 2026.

    Abstract

    AI-assisted cyber situational awareness triggers machine-generated reasoning traces (step-by-step justifications for anomaly classifications) that a human operator is expected to review. Because cyber signals and their traces arrive faster than any operator can process, human review is the limiting constraint…

    AI-assisted cyber situational awareness triggers machine-generated reasoning traces (step-by-step justifications for anomaly classifications) that a human operator is expected to review. Because cyber signals and their traces arrive faster than any operator can process, human review is the limiting constraint on oversight. The standard approach is to identify the riskiest cyber events for review using model-side signals such as confidence or uncertainty. That framing ignores the operator's cognitive capacity which varies sharply with the operational environment. We propose an alternative where the system's environmental and connectivity telemetry serves as an available, non-invasive proxy for operator load. That same telemetry determines whether the human-AI partnership can reach the broader collective for support. In a maritime platform, environmental and connectivity attributes including depth, number of active communications paths, density of the tracked contact picture, and operational tempo all carry this signal. Need for operator oversight becomes a decision that materializes as a combination of both risk and environment-derived operator capacity. We present a reference architecture for a connectedness-aware oversight engine, demonstrating everyday use cases alongside its intended incorporation into the submarine cyber-defense toolkit. Two themes emerge: 1) the operator’s environmental state is itself a connectedness measurement, and 2) connectedness drives the cognitive load and defines a collective boundary in human-AI cyber operations.

    Summary: Connectedness, Cognitive Load, and Human-AI Oversight in Cyber Operations

    U.S. Navy sailors have to manage the ship, carry out the mission, monitor tactical systems, and respond to cybersecurity threats at the same time. During demanding operations, this creates a heavy mental workload, and current cyber displays can add even more information for sailors to process.

    AI can help by detecting unusual cyber activity and explaining why it believes something may be a threat. However, these explanations can arrive faster than a sailor can review them. The goal is to make AI-supported cyber defense more practical by matching oversight demands to the sailor’s real operating environment and available support at that particular moment.

    Regulating Attention in Digital Platforms via Dynamic Taxation and Strategic Learning

    Abhishek Umrawal (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign), Eliot Robson (Narmi Inc.), Alex Zheng (Independent Researcher), Elaine Ly (Northeastern University), Lav Varshney (Stony Brook University)

    Abstract

    We study the problem of mitigating information overload in large-scale digital ecosystems through dynamically learned taxation mechanisms. We model the interaction between a social planner and strategic message senders as a Stackelberg game, where the planner (leader) sets a tax…

    We study the problem of mitigating information overload in large-scale digital ecosystems through dynamically learned taxation mechanisms. We model the interaction between a social planner and strategic message senders as a Stackelberg game, where the planner (leader) sets a tax policy on messaging and the senders (followers) adapt their behavior over time via reinforcement learning under limited interaction horizons, giving rise to non-equilibrium and boundedly rational behavior. The planner’s objective balances reducing aggregate message volume with promoting equity in access to user attention, formalized via a log-participation reward and the Gini coefficient. Leveraging a query-based follower oracle, we show that the leader’s policy optimization problem can be cast as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), enabling the use of reinforcement learning to search over tax policies. In the one-step single-sender, one-step multi-sender, and multi-step single-sender environments, RL-trained tax policies reach or closely approximate the analytically computed optimum, including exact, stable convergence to the dynamic-programming optimum in the multi-step setting under our best hyperparameter configuration; we additionally observe that other hyperparameter choices in the multi-step setting reach a comparable peak but subsequently decay to a majority of it, a sensitivity we have not yet fully characterized. The multi-step, multi-sender environment is in progress. These results suggest that dynamic taxation, framed as a POMDP over learning-based follower responses, is a viable and promising approach to regulating information overload in attention-constrained environments.

    The Market for Synthetic Lemons: What Can George Akerlof Teach Us About Safeguarding Cognitive Security?

    Dennis Murphy (Georgia Tech), Jon Lindsay

    Abstract

    Governments, industry, and academia have all remarked on the potential for generative artificial intelligence to greatly enhance the efficacy of adversarial influence operations by driving down the cost of convincing synthetic media. So far, this threat has not been realized.…

    Governments, industry, and academia have all remarked on the potential for generative artificial intelligence to greatly enhance the efficacy of adversarial influence operations by driving down the cost of convincing synthetic media. So far, this threat has not been realized. Significant research has explored facets of this conundrum, but there remains room for frameworks that explain why. This paper puts forward George Akerlof’s analysis of markets under quality uncertainty. Akerlof showed how information asymmetries can bring down a market; he also showed why they usually do not. Guarantees, licensing, and brand names establish verification externally, beyond the interaction between a customer and the purveyor of potentially dubious goods. This paper contends that similar mechanisms can be applied to synthetic media, and it identifies four pathways for securing collective sensemaking against GAI-enabled synthetic media: technological, institutional, legal, and social. It considers each against the influence operations that targeted the 2024 United States presidential election, an episode in which abundant synthetic media produced no observable collapse of the information environment. It also acknowledges substantial limitations: disinformation may not work as commonly assumed, audiences may respond to uncertainty by discounting everything rather than believing falsehoods, some audiences may prefer lemons, and verification mechanisms are themselves fragile.

    The Accountability Regime as Cognitive Infrastructure: How Organizational Design Determines Whether Cognitive Security Interventions Function

    Shefali Patil (University of Texas at Austin)

    Abstract

    Cognitive security is the “integrity of the conditions under which people and groups reason for themselves.” Research in this area has concentrated at two levels: the cognitive vulnerabilities of individuals, and the design of the systems and interfaces those individuals encounter. Both are necessary, but this position paper argues that a third level determines whether interventions at the first two can reliably function in organizations: the accountability regime that surrounds the human-AI interface. Critical infrastructure organizations have historically maintained two accountability regimes simultaneously: procedural, which emphasize shared procedural standards, documented workflows, and coordinated adherence to established protocols, and discretionary, which emphasize and protect professional judgment through designated roles that enable professionals to exercise tacit, contextual knowledge. Drawing on organizational research on accountability and comments I recently submitted to NIST on its Trustworthy AI in Critical Infrastructure Profile, I argue that AI is dismantling the organization’s ability to maintain both regimes. This collapse produces two opposing failure modes—mindless conformity and reckless deviation—that map directly onto this community’s concern with over- and under-reliance. I offer a diagnostic (the asymmetry test), three design principles, and a research agenda that treats the accountability regime as a moderator of every mitigation the Cognitive Vulnerability and Mitigation Catalog will contain.

    Shaping Deepfake Non-Consensual Intimate Imagery Prevention and Intervention Strategies through Frontline Perspectives

    Riya Ranjan (Stanford University)

    Content noteNon-consensual intimate imagery and image-based sexual abuse.

    Abstract

    Deepfake non-consensual intimate imagery is the dominant real-world use of generative image technology, yet it has received comparatively little attention from the technical AI community. Where deepfake NCII has been studied, it has largely been treated as an intellectual-property problem to be met with existing privacy and copyright law—a framing that neglects both its nature as a form of sexual violence and the expertise of the frontline that already responds to it. Reframing deepfake NCII as technology-facilitated sexual violence, I draw on a pilot study of semi-structured interviews with twelve U.S.-based sexual violence service providers to make two contributions. First, I characterize the frontline of deepfake NCII response as it currently operates, identifying four concerns: insufficient specialized training, scarce resources, the interlocking failure of under-legislation and under-enforcement, and a desire for stronger collaborative pipelines and prevention. Second, I synthesize these findings into a framework for frontline-centered AI governance, which treats the intervention and prevention workers who encounter harm first as constitutive participants in how AI systems are governed rather than as downstream recipients of governance decisions. I argue that this framework generalizes beyond deepfake NCII to other domains of AI safety.

    Collective Cognitive Security by Design: What Makes Human Groups Cognitively Secure and Agentic in AI-Rich Environments?

    Generative and agentic AI are becoming embedded in the cognitive infrastructure of work, education, and decision-making. As AI increasingly frames problems, generates alternatives, evaluates options, and shapes decisions, the cognitive-security challenge extends beyond inaccurate or manipulative information. Even when AI performs well, two related vulnerabilities may emerge: cognitive substitution, in which people have fewer opportunities to retrieve, generate, question, evaluate, and own judgments themselves; and premature AI framing, in which a shared machine-generated interpretation organizes collective reasoning before uniquely held human knowledge has surfaced.

    We use Collective Cognitive Security (CCS) to denote the capacity of a group to preserve the conditions under which its members can independently generate, challenge, integrate, own, and act on judgments while interacting with AI. CCS is distinct from collective intelligence: a group may achieve a high-quality outcome while remaining cognitively insecure if human participants no longer meaningfully contribute to, evaluate, challenge, or own the reasoning that produced it.

    We propose Protected Human Divergence as a candidate design principle, Designed Collective Thinking (DCT) as a candidate social architecture, and the Epistemic Agency Practice Loop (EAPL) as a candidate mechanism through which epistemic agency may be repeatedly enacted. We examine how deliberation structure and AI-entry timing affect enacted agency, cognitive diversity, idiosyncratic knowledge expression, decision ownership, responsibility, idea provenance, AI reliance, and cognitive-capability retention.

    The framework implies a concrete mitigation and measurement strategy: protect a phase of human generation before shared AI framing, then examine whether uniquely held human information enters collective reasoning and whether people continue to generate, challenge, evaluate, choose, and own the resulting judgment. The broader question is how human-AI systems can augment performance without eroding the human capacities, expertise, and collective knowledge on which resilient organizations and societies depend.

    Collective Cognitive Security by Design

    Dr. Anat Levy-Raz, University of Haifa. What makes human groups cognitively secure and agentic in AI-rich environments?

    1. Core distinction

    “You can outsource parts of thinking. You cannot outsource understanding.”

    AI can assist: summarize, calculate, translate, synthesize, generate options. Humans must retain: understanding, context, critical evaluation, judgment, ownership and responsibility.

    The vulnerability begins when assistance quietly becomes substitution for human judgment.

    2. Two related vulnerabilities

    Cognitive substitution: AI replaces cognitive acts humans would otherwise exercise.

    Premature AI framing: A shared AI-generated frame organizes collective reasoning before uniquely held human knowledge surfaces.

    3. Target construct

    Collective Cognitive Security (CCS): The capacity of a group to preserve the conditions under which its members can independently generate, challenge, integrate, own, and act on judgments while interacting with AI.

    High task performance does not guarantee human cognitive security.

    The missing layer is process integrity: who retrieved, questioned, evaluated, chose, and owned the reasoning? It asks whether people still: generate, challenge, evaluate, integrate, choose, own, act.

    4. Four different questions
    • Psychological Safety: Can I speak?
    • Epistemic Agency: Can I generate, judge, and own?
    • Collective Intelligence: How well can the group perform?
    • Collective Cognitive Security: Can humans remain epistemic actors while AI participates?
    5. The evidence gap

    Human + AI is not automatically the best system. Augmentation is not the same as synergy.

    Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone (2024): across 106 experiments, Human+AI often improved over humans alone, but did not on average outperform the best available component.

    Interaction architecture matters.

    6. Design hypothesis

    AI timing and interaction architecture are cognitive-security design variables.

    • Protected Human Divergence: candidate design principle.
    • Designed Collective Thinking (DCT): candidate social architecture.
    • Epistemic Agency Practice Loop (EAPL): candidate mechanism / process model.

    Sequence: independent human retrieval, then protected human divergence, then structured human deliberation, then AI augmentation, then human re-evaluation, then human ownership and action.

    Human First does not mean Human Only. This is a testable candidate architecture, not a prescription.

    7. What to measure
    • uniquely held human knowledge surfaced
    • enacted epistemic participation
    • cognitive diversity
    • decision ownership
    • responsibility
    • idea provenance
    • AI reliance
    • cognitive-capability retention
    8. Cognitive Vulnerability & Mitigation Catalog

    Vulnerability: premature AI framing and cognitive substitution can reduce human epistemic participation and prevent uniquely held human knowledge from entering collective reasoning.

    Mitigation / governance hook: protect a phase of human generation before consequential shared AI framing.

    Metric: uniquely held human knowledge surfaced + enacted epistemic participation.

    Not only humans in the loop. Humans developing in the loop.

    A Two-Axis Taxonomy for Cognitive Security: Timing, Source, and the Case for Treating Capacity Erosion Separately

    Kristina Kempkey (Institute for AI Policy and Strategy), Matthew Gentzel (Longview Philanthropy)

    Abstract

    Cognitive security concerns the integrity of attention, perception, memory, and judgment that individuals and institutions depend on to interpret the world and act effectively. AI-enabled manipulation, such as through generative models and recommender systems, has become a central focus of…

    Cognitive security concerns the integrity of attention, perception, memory, and judgment that individuals and institutions depend on to interpret the world and act effectively. AI-enabled manipulation, such as through generative models and recommender systems, has become a central focus of research, funding, and media coverage, often involving a deliberate attacker influencing a single, high-stakes decision. In contrast, this paper categorizes threats using two axes. The first axis, timing, distinguishes between acute capture, where a decision is hijacked within a single window, and chronic drift, where incremental nudges accumulate and gradually distort subsequent decisions. The second axis, source, differentiates adversarial harm, which involves an intentional attacker, from emergent harm, which arises from systemic incentives without deliberate intent. Each of the four resulting categories is based on a distinct threat model and requires tailored defenses, and two of them remain underexplored. Additionally, cognitive offloading exists outside this framework. Cognitive offloading, often accelerated by AI systems that automate reasoning or decision-making, does not redirect decisions toward incorrect outcomes but instead diminishes the capacity for independent decision-making. Most manipulation-focused defenses do not address it. We outline how each category can be measured, identify where the axes may obscure certain problems, and propose priorities for future research agendas.

    The Field Guide to Thinking for Ourselves is a semi-automated, human-curated guide to cognitive security and other aspects of human cognition in the age of AI. Its goal is to be a friendly guide to the multi-disciplinary, rapidly moving efforts around protecting human cognition. The Guide is built on top of a property graph, curated by humans and fed by machines. If successful, the Guide will provide common ground for diverse convenings and help advise funders new to this important domain.
